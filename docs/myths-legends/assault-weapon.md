## Description

The "Assault Weapon" is one of the most talked-about creatures in America and one of the hardest to pin down. Naturalists agree on almost nothing about it: what it is, where it came from, or how to tell it apart from its cousins. Even sympathetic observers admit the problem. A 1988 study that helped make the creature famous warned that defining one "is not easy" and that it is "extremely difficult to develop a legal definition that restricts the availability of 'assault weapons' without affecting legitimate semi-automatic guns."[^vpc]

## Taxonomy: not to be confused with the "assault rifle"

Its nearest look-alike is the **"assault rifle"**, a *selective-fire* military rifle that can shoot fully automatic. Automatic weapons have been tightly regulated under federal law since the **National Firearms Act of 1934**, and further restricted by the Firearm Owners Protection Act of 1986.[^wiki] The "Assault Weapon", by contrast, is defined in law as a **semi-automatic** firearm, which fires one round per trigger pull.[^koper] As Justice Kavanaugh has noted, "AR–15s are semi-automatic, but so too are most handguns."[^kav] Laws defining "assault weapons" don't cover fully automatic arms at all.[^wiki]

The confusion is no accident. The 1988 study said so itself:

> "The weapons' menacing looks, coupled with the public's confusion over fully automatic machine guns versus semi-automatic 'assault weapons'—anything that looks like a machine gun is assumed to be a machine gun—can only increase the chance of public support for restrictions on these weapons."[^vpc]

## Origin myth

The creature's origin is disputed, as befits a legend.

- **Early sightings:** a 1978 Kansas newspaper ad used a version of the term for semi-automatic rifles, and a 1985 California Assembly bill sought to ban semi-automatic "assault firearms." Some writers argue the gun industry first used the name as a marketing term.[^wiki]
- **The great popularization (1988–1989):** many credit the term's political breakout to the Violence Policy Center's 1988 study ***"Assault Weapons" and Accessories in America***, widely attributed to VPC founder **Josh Sugarmann**, and to the reaction after the **January 17, 1989** schoolyard shooting in Stockton, California.[^wiki][^vpc][^koper] The study predicted the creature would become "the leading topic of America's gun control debate."[^vpc] It added that targeting it is "not merely a matter of going after guns that are 'black and wicked looking.'"[^vpc] Field researchers have spent 38 years checking.
- **First enclosures:** a 1989 federal ban on importing certain semi-automatic rifles followed, along with state bans in California, New Jersey, Connecticut and Hawaii between 1989 and 1993.[^koper]

## Field marks: how to identify one (1994 edition)

Under the federal ban of **September 13, 1994**, a semi-automatic rifle that accepts a detachable magazine was an "assault weapon" if it showed **two or more** of these field marks:[^koper]

1. A folding or telescoping stock
2. A pistol grip that protrudes beneath the action
3. A bayonet mount
4. A flash suppressor, or a threaded barrel designed to take one
5. A grenade launcher

The law also banned **18 models and variations by name**, plus revolving-cylinder shotguns. The ATF eventually counted **118** covered models and variations.[^koper]

!!! example "Field guide tip"
    Note what is *not* on the list: caliber, power, rate of fire and range. The creature is identified entirely by its plumage. The Justice Department's own evaluation said the law "targets a relatively small number of weapons based on features that have little to do with the weapons' operation, and removing those features is sufficient to make the weapons legal."[^koper]

## Molting

The "Assault Weapon" is famous for **molting**. When ordered to change, it sheds a feather or two and walks right back into the store. In the words of the federally funded study:

> "Relatively cosmetic changes, such as removing a flash hider or bayonet mount, are sufficient to transform a banned weapon into a legal substitute."[^koper]

One well-documented specimen, the **Colt Match Target H-Bar**, was described as "essentially identical to pre-ban versions of the AR-15," minus accessories like a flash hider, threaded barrel or bayonet lug.[^koper]

## Lifespan in federal captivity

The federal enclosure lasted **ten years**. It took effect in September 1994 and expired by its own sunset clause in **September 2004**.[^koper] Its own evaluators predicted that, if renewed, "the ban's effects on gun violence are likely to be small at best and perhaps too small for reliable measurement."[^koper] It was not renewed. A 2013 Senate bill to bring it back failed.[^wiki]

## Diet and habitat: is it really a predator?

Here the legend and the record part ways.

- Before the 1994 ban, "assault weapons" "were used in only a small fraction of gun crimes … about 2% according to most studies and no more than 8%." Most of those were **pistols**, not rifles.[^koper]
- In 2024, the FBI recorded **401** murders with **rifles of all types**, out of **15,795**. That's about **2.5%**, and it includes every deer rifle, .22 and lever gun along with the AR-15s. That same year, **633** people were killed with hands, fists and feet, and **1,566** with knives.[^fbi]

So the creature is spotted constantly on television and rarely in the crime tables. For the full numbers, see **[Narrative vs. Reality: "'Assault weapons' are the weapon of choice"](../narrative-vs-reality/assault-weapons-weapon-of-choice.md)**.

## Population

The AR-15, the species most often identified as the "Assault Weapon", is extremely common. In 2025, Justice Kavanaugh wrote that "Americans today possess an estimated 20 to 30 million AR–15s," legal in 41 of the 50 states.[^kav] A creature that common, kept peacefully by tens of millions of households, would be called **livestock** in most field guides.

## Current status: before the Court

On **June 30, 2026**, the U.S. Supreme Court agreed to decide "whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee the right to possess AR-15 platform and similar semiautomatic rifles," in *Viramontes v. Cook County* (consolidated with *Grant v. Higgins*, Connecticut's ban). Argument is set for **December 2, 2026**.[^scotus]

## Ranger Lock's notes

> "Folks keep telling me they've seen one. I ask what made it an 'assault weapon.' They say 'the grip,' 'the stock' or 'it was black.' Then I show them my granddad's semi-auto deer rifle, same action and same trigger, and they say, 'Oh, that's fine.' Partner, that's no species. That's a paint job."
> (Ranger Lock, Colofornia Dreaming Wildlife Service. *Lock is fictional. The rifles are real.*)
